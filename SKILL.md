# Smart Brevity

## Purpose

Produce stakeholder-facing writing that lets a busy reader answer, in order:

1. What changed, happened, or needs a decision?
2. Why does it matter to this audience?
3. What evidence, context, or risk do they need?
4. What should happen next, by whom, and when?

Apply editorial judgment, not mechanical shortening. The goal is the shortest complete communication, not the fewest words.

## Non-negotiable standard

- **Lead with the news.** Put the decision, result, change, risk, or customer impact in the first sentence.
- **Make relevance explicit.** Use a concise **Why it matters** section written for the intended reader.
- **Prioritize support.** Keep only the facts that help the reader understand, decide, or act; order them by importance.
- **Make action concrete.** State the request, owner, and deadline when relevant. Say **No action required** when that is useful.
- **Preserve the truth burden.** Keep material qualifiers, uncertainty, warnings, exceptions, scope, and source distinctions.
- **Write for scanning.** Use short paragraphs, informative labels, selective bolding, and a small number of focused bullets.
- **Stop when the job is done.** Do not repeat the lead in different words or add a ceremonial conclusion.

## Decide whether to use this format

Use Smart Brevity as the primary format for tactical, stakeholder-facing communication such as:

- executive and board updates;
- project status, milestone, risk, and dependency updates;
- decisions, policy changes, launches, and organizational announcements;
- meeting recaps centered on decisions and actions;
- customer notices and service-incident updates;
- concise summaries that accompany a longer source of truth.

Do **not** let brevity govern material where completeness or exact language must lead. This includes contracts, regulatory filings, legal notices, compliance evidence, safety procedures, medical guidance, security runbooks, deeply technical specifications, and step-by-step operating instructions.

For high-stakes or detail-heavy material, use Smart Brevity only as a summary layer. Keep the authoritative text, procedure, analysis, or record intact and clearly available. Do not rewrite mandatory language unless explicitly authorized. Do not use a terse style when empathy, consultation, or careful explanation is itself part of the task.

Do not claim Axios endorsement, certification, or official status for this skill or its output.

## Establish the communication contract

Before drafting, identify these inputs from the request and source material:

- **Audience:** Who will read this, and what do they already know?
- **Purpose:** Inform, request action, secure a decision, align, reassure, or document?
- **News:** What is newly true, changed, decided, at risk, or unresolved?
- **Impact:** What consequence matters to this audience?
- **Support:** Which facts, metrics, tradeoffs, risks, or dependencies make the claim credible?
- **Next step:** What action, decision, owner, deadline, or next update is needed?
- **Constraints:** Which qualifications, warnings, required wording, confidentiality limits, or unknowns must remain?

If a critical input is absent, do not invent it. Omit the unsupported field, mark the gap plainly, or request the missing fact when interaction permits.

## Default information architecture

Use the following structure unless the channel or audience calls for a lighter version.

### 1. Subject or title

Write a specific, outcome-oriented title, usually 3-10 words. Start with the strongest concrete noun or verb. Prefer the result over the topic.

- Strong: `Launch delayed two weeks by vendor defect`
- Weak: `Project Phoenix update`

Do not use clickbait, vague urgency, or a title that overstates the evidence.

### 2. Lead

In one or two sentences, state the news and its current status. Include material scope or uncertainty in the lead rather than hiding it later.

Lead with:

- the decision for an announcement;
- status versus plan for a project update;
- the requested choice for a decision memo;
- current customer impact for an incident;
- decisions and commitments for a meeting recap.

Avoid warm-up phrases such as “I wanted to share,” “As you may know,” or a chronology that delays the point.

### 3. Why it matters

Use the label **Why it matters:** followed by the audience-specific consequence in one or two sentences.

A strong impact statement answers at least one of these questions:

- What decision does this change?
- What risk, cost, time, revenue, customer, or operational outcome is affected?
- What will this audience need to do differently?
- Why does the timing matter?

Do not merely restate the lead, use generic language such as “This is important,” or invent business impact that the evidence does not support.

### 4. Supporting blocks

Use two to five prioritized bullets or short labeled blocks when possible. Each should carry one idea and begin with a meaningful lead-in.

Useful labels include:

- **By the numbers**
- **What changed**
- **Progress**
- **Risk**
- **Tradeoff**
- **What we know**
- **What we do not know**
- **Customer impact**
- **Decision criteria**

Write the lead-in so a scanner can understand the point without reading every word. Group or move secondary details rather than producing a long, flat bullet list.

### 5. Action, decision, or next update

Use the label that matches the job:

- **Action:** `[Owner or audience] will [specific action] by [date/time].`
- **Decision needed:** `Choose [option or decision] by [date/time]; recommendation: [choice and reason].`
- **What's next:** `[Owner] will [next milestone] by [date/time].`
- **Next update:** `We will update [audience/channel] by [date/time], even if the status is unchanged.`
- **No action required:** Use only when readers might otherwise wonder.

Use absolute dates and include a time zone when timing is operationally important.

### 6. Go deeper

Add **Go deeper:** only when readers need a path to the full source, appendix, ticket, dashboard, runbook, decision record, or technical explanation. The concise update must still stand on its own.

Do not use **Go deeper** as a dumping ground for facts that are necessary to understand the lead, impact, risk, or action.

## Workflow: create a new draft

1. **Inventory the facts.** Capture decisions, dates, numbers, names, commitments, risks, qualifiers, warnings, unknowns, and source links.
2. **Choose one reader outcome.** Decide what the primary audience should know, decide, feel, or do after reading.
3. **Write the lead from the facts.** State the most consequential new information without setup.
4. **Write the audience impact.** Explain the practical implication, not merely why the author cares.
5. **Select supporting evidence.** Keep the smallest set of facts that makes the update credible and actionable.
6. **Add the next step.** Name the action or decision, owner, deadline, and next update as applicable.
7. **Move optional depth.** Put necessary secondary context under **Go deeper** or in the linked source of truth.
8. **Run the review.** Use [the review checklist](references/review-checklist.md) before finalizing consequential communication.

When there are several equally important developments, either split them into separate updates or write one umbrella lead and give each development a clearly labeled block. Do not pretend multiple priorities are one “big thing.”

## Workflow: transform an existing draft

1. **Create a preservation ledger.** List every material fact, number, date, commitment, qualifier, warning, exception, dependency, dissenting view, and unresolved question.
2. **Find the buried lead.** Identify the sentence that changes what the reader knows, decides, or does. Rewrite it as the opening.
3. **Separate news from background.** Keep essential context near the point; move optional history to **Go deeper**; remove familiar or irrelevant setup.
4. **Rebuild the hierarchy.** Add **Why it matters**, group related evidence, and order support by decision value rather than source order.
5. **Turn activity into outcomes.** Replace “we met, discussed, and reviewed” with what was decided, learned, completed, blocked, or requested.
6. **Make ownership visible.** Convert passive or collective language into named owners, actions, and dates when the source supports them.
7. **Compress sentence by sentence.** Remove filler, repetition, abstract nouns, and unnecessary hedging without strengthening the claim.
8. **Reconcile with the ledger.** Confirm that every decision-relevant item remains accurate and visible. Flag source conflicts instead of silently choosing one.
9. **Review the result.** Read only the title, lead, labels, and bold text; they should communicate the news, impact, and next step.

Do not delete a detail merely because it is long. Delete it when it is unnecessary; move it when it is necessary but secondary.

## Adapt to the audience

| Audience | Lead with | Include | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Executives | Outcome, decision, material variance, or risk | Business impact, key metric, tradeoff, confidence, recommendation | Activity logs and implementation detail that do not change a decision |
| Project stakeholders | Status versus plan and the most important change | Milestones, dependencies, blockers, owner, deadline, decision needed | A flat list of completed tasks with no implication |
| Teams | What changes for them and when | Responsibilities, sequence, support, exceptions, where to ask questions | Management euphemisms, unexplained rationale, or hidden workload |
| Customers | Current customer impact and available path forward | Scope, status, workaround, empathy, next update | Internal jargon, blame, speculation, unsupported restoration times |
| Incident audiences | Current impact, severity, and response state | Confirmed facts, mitigation, uncertainty, affected scope, next timestamp | Premature root cause, false certainty, noisy investigation detail |

Match tone to consequence. Be direct without being cold. For bad news, pair the factual lead with proportionate acknowledgment and practical help; do not bury impact beneath reassurance.

## Writing rules

- Use plain, concrete language and active voice.
- Keep one idea per paragraph; most paragraphs should be one or two sentences.
- Use bullets for parallel facts, not for every sentence.
- Use bold for labels or the decisive phrase, not for decoration.
- Prefer exact numbers, dates, units, and named owners over vague quantifiers.
- Distinguish a **decision**, **recommendation**, **proposal**, **estimate**, **target**, and **confirmed fact**.
- Attribute material claims when the source or owner matters.
- Use consistent terms for the same thing; do not trade precision for variety.
- Avoid filler, throat-clearing, slogans, rhetorical questions, inflated adjectives, and unnecessary emojis.
- Do not target an arbitrary word count. End at the shortest length that preserves comprehension and sound decisions.

## Accuracy, uncertainty, and safety guardrails

- Never manufacture a metric, cause, deadline, owner, customer impact, consensus, or rationale.
- Preserve uncertainty where it changes the decision. Use calibrated language such as “confirmed,” “likely,” “estimated,” “not yet verified,” or “unknown.”
- Keep the scope and denominator of numbers. Do not present a partial result as a total.
- Preserve warnings, contraindications, exceptions, access limitations, and rollback conditions.
- Do not convert correlation into causation or a target into a commitment.
- Do not promise an incident resolution time without reliable support.
- Keep controlling legal, compliance, safety, and policy language intact; a summary cannot supersede it.
- If sources conflict, identify the conflict and the evidence needed to resolve it.
- If sensitive information is unnecessary for the reader's task, exclude it rather than making it more prominent.

## Output behavior

Unless the user asks for commentary, return the polished communication rather than an explanation of the method.

- For a new draft, choose the closest pattern in [the templates](references/templates.md).
- For a rewrite, preserve the source's meaning and material detail; do not add claims solely to make the structure feel complete.
- For high-stakes material, provide a concise summary plus an explicit path to the authoritative detail.
- When structure is uncertain, consult [the worked examples](references/examples.md).

## Completion test

The communication is ready when:

- the first sentence delivers the news;
- **Why it matters** is specific to the audience;
- a scanner can follow the title, labels, and bold text;
- supporting points are prioritized rather than exhaustive;
- action, owner, deadline, decision, or next update is explicit when relevant;
- every material qualifier and warning from the source remains;
- the full read supplies enough context for a sound decision without unnecessary repetition.
