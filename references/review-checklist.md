# Review Checklist

Run this after drafting and again after any substantial compression. For high-stakes communication, compare the final draft directly with the source material.

## 1. News and lead

- [ ] The title names a result, change, risk, decision, or impact rather than only a topic.
- [ ] The first sentence delivers the news without warm-up or chronology.
- [ ] The lead distinguishes confirmed facts from proposals, forecasts, estimates, and hypotheses.
- [ ] Material scope or uncertainty appears early enough to prevent a misleading first impression.
- [ ] If several developments compete for attention, they are split or grouped under an honest umbrella lead.

## 2. Why it matters

- [ ] **Why it matters** is written for the intended audience, not the author.
- [ ] It names a concrete implication: decision, risk, cost, time, customer impact, operational effect, or required behavior.
- [ ] It does not merely restate the lead.
- [ ] It does not use vague claims such as “This is important” or “This will drive value.”
- [ ] Every claimed implication is supported by the source or clearly labeled as an inference.

## 3. Supporting detail

- [ ] The supporting points are ordered by decision value, not by the order in which events occurred.
- [ ] Each bullet carries one idea and begins with an informative lead-in.
- [ ] The list is small enough to scan; long lists are grouped, prioritized, or moved to **Go deeper**.
- [ ] Metrics include the period, unit, comparison, denominator, and scope needed to interpret them.
- [ ] Activity language has been converted into outcomes, findings, decisions, blockers, or requests.
- [ ] Background remains only when it changes comprehension or action.

## 4. Action and ownership

- [ ] The draft states the decision, action, or next step when one exists.
- [ ] Each action has one accountable owner or clearly defined audience.
- [ ] Deadlines use absolute dates; operational times include a time zone.
- [ ] A decision request includes the choice, deadline, and recommendation when appropriate.
- [ ] Incident communication names the next update time, even when resolution timing is unknown.
- [ ] **No action required** appears when readers would otherwise be uncertain.

## 5. Accuracy and over-compression audit

Compare the final draft with the source and confirm that it preserves:

- [ ] names, numbers, dates, units, and commitments;
- [ ] scope, sample size, denominator, and baseline;
- [ ] uncertainty, confidence, assumptions, and data limitations;
- [ ] warnings, exceptions, contraindications, and rollback conditions;
- [ ] dependencies, dissent, unresolved questions, and material tradeoffs;
- [ ] the distinction between correlation and cause;
- [ ] the distinction between a target, estimate, recommendation, proposal, and commitment;
- [ ] controlling legal, compliance, safety, policy, or technical language outside the summary;
- [ ] links or paths to the authoritative source when secondary detail was moved.

Stop and correct the draft if it is shorter but could cause a reasonable reader to make a different or less-informed decision than the source supports.

## 6. Style and scan test

- [ ] Paragraphs contain one idea and are usually one or two sentences.
- [ ] Language is plain, concrete, and active.
- [ ] Bold text is selective and meaningful.
- [ ] Bullets are parallel and not used for every sentence.
- [ ] Filler, repetition, slogans, inflated adjectives, and throat-clearing are gone.
- [ ] Tone is direct but proportionate; bad news includes appropriate acknowledgment without hiding the facts.
- [ ] Reading only the title, lead, labels, and bold phrases reveals the news, impact, and next step.
- [ ] The ending does not repeat the opening or add an empty conclusion.

## Common failure modes and repairs

| Failure | What it looks like | Repair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Buried lead | Background, meeting history, or process appears before the change | Move the decision, result, risk, or impact to sentence one |
| Filler | “I wanted to reach out,” “As you know,” or ceremonial thanks delays the point | Delete the warm-up; keep only proportionate human acknowledgment |
| Vague impact | “This is important” or “This creates value” | Name the affected decision, customer, metric, risk, cost, time, or behavior |
| Excessive bullets | Eight or more equal-weight items with no hierarchy | Keep the top items, group related facts, or move secondary material to **Go deeper** |
| Unsupported claim | The draft adds cause, confidence, savings, consensus, or urgency not present in the source | Remove it, qualify it, attribute it, or mark it as an inference |
| Missing next step | Readers understand the update but not what happens now | Add action, owner, deadline, decision request, or next-update time |
| Over-compression | Scope, caveat, warning, exception, or tradeoff disappears | Restore it near the claim or retain the authoritative detail alongside the summary |
| Activity dump | The update lists meetings, reviews, and effort | Replace activity with what changed, completed, failed, was learned, or remains blocked |
| Repeated lead | The title, opening, impact, and conclusion say the same thing | Give each section a distinct job and delete the ceremonial conclusion |
| False precision | A tentative forecast becomes a committed date or an estimate becomes an exact figure | Restore calibrated language and the evidence boundary |

## Final release test

Ask four questions:

1. **News:** What is newly true?
2. **Impact:** Why does this audience care?
3. **Evidence:** What must they know to trust and interpret it?
4. **Action:** What happens next, by whom, and when?

If any answer is missing, vague, unsupported, or hidden, revise before sending.
